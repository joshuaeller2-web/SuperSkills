# Super Decision Council

Use different perspectives to expose decision-relevant disagreement. The output is a better decision, not a long roleplay transcript.

## Frame the decision

Identify the choice, desired outcome, constraints, deadline, available evidence, and reversibility. Include keeping the current approach when it is a real option. Use known context; ask only about an uncertainty that could change the recommendation.

Separate factual uncertainty from differences in priorities. A council cannot resolve an unknown market fact merely by voting.

## Choose contrasting lenses

Select only the lenses useful to the decision, normally three to five:

- User value and clarity: what problem is solved, for whom, and with what complexity?
- Execution and systems: what dependencies, capacity, mechanisms, and operating costs determine feasibility?
- Risk and economics: what downside, incentives, irreversibility, and opportunity cost matter?
- Growth and offer: where is demand evidence, adoption friction, or weak positioning?
- Skeptical evidence: what assumptions are unsupported, and what result would falsify the preferred option?

Use domain-specific lenses when these do not fit. Do not impersonate real people or present imagined advisers as endorsements.

## Run the comparison honestly

When independent agent work is requested or otherwise authorized and available, brief agents with the same raw decision facts and distinct questions. Let them form an initial view before sharing competing conclusions. Do not send unnecessary personal or confidential information to external services.

Otherwise apply the lenses sequentially in the current agent and label them as one agent's perspectives. Do not claim independent experts, model diversity, or consensus that did not occur. If an agent fails, mark its view unavailable; do not silently replace it with a simulated participant.

For each lens, identify the strongest case, strongest objection, key assumption, and a practical test. Resolve factual disputes through evidence when feasible. Keep value disagreements visible rather than averaging them into a false agreement.

## Synthesize

Give a supported recommendation, its rationale, the strongest counterargument, the assumption or condition that would reverse the choice, and the next useful test or action. Use an options table when comparison is clearer than prose. Use scores only when the criteria, weights, and evidence justify them; do not manufacture numerical certainty.

Scale the depth to the decision. A small reversible experiment does not require repeated debate rounds. The recommendation does not itself authorize spending, sending messages, or implementing an external change.
